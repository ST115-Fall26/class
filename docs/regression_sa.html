# Goal: report the largset positive and largest negative residual with the obs

\(\hat{\text{flipper length}} = 127.33 + 1.67*\text{bill length}\)

For a 1 mm increase in bill length, we estimate the mean of flipper length to increase by 1.67mm.

a <- predict(m1, newdata = data.frame(bill_length_mm = 40))

\(\hat{\text{flipper length}} = 127.33 + 1.67*\text{40}\)

The estimated mean flipper length when bill length is 40mm is equal to 194.28.

Statistical Inference

\(Ho: \beta_1 = 0\)

\(Ha: \beta_1 \neq 0\)

Assumptions

– independence

Comes down to how we sample! Collect data through a simple random sample.

plot(m1, which = 1)
plot(m1, which = 2)

Note: From our residual vs fitted plot, we don’t have strong evidence to reject, the equal variance assumption. Further, we don’t see a non-obvious linear trend in our residuals vs fitted (checking for linearity).

Note: We do see some evidence that our assumption of normality is violated, as our Normal Q-Q plot has heavy tails that deviate from the line. However, our sample size is very large, and we are robust to this assumption.

With a t-statistic of 15.69 and a p-value of < 0.001 from a t-distribution with 331 degrees of freedom, we reject the null hypothesis that there is no population linear relationship between bill length and flipper length.

Confidence Intervals

We make confidence intervals to estimate plausible values of our population parameter! In fact, we can create three different confidence intervals depending on our research question:

– One for \(\beta_1\) - population slope

– \(\mu_\text{Y|X}\) - population mean

– \(y\) - individual observation

Population Slope

confint(m1, level = 0.95)
                   2.5 %     97.5 %
